Validation Testing Report

Bottom line

We tested three health behaviour concepts with 21 young people and parents. Concept 2 (Hook U Up), a peer challenge card deck, had the strongest youth response and is closest to pilot-ready after fixing its proof mechanic. Concept 1 (Bedtime Bundle) works for families where sleep structure is slipping, but needs a re-engagement loop. Concept 3 (We Open Up) needs a physical pilot before any design decisions, as the core mechanic could not be tested online.

We tested three early-stage concepts with 21 participants, 12 parents and 9 youth, over individual online sessions from 9 to 13 March 2026. Each concept tackles a different moment in a young person's day: bedtime, after-school time with friends, and family conversation at home. None of them were finished products. They were prototypes, built just enough to put in front of real people and find out what holds up and what falls apart.

The goal was practical: understand whether each concept's core mechanic actually works when someone who has never seen it before tries to use it. We wanted to know what resonates, what confuses, and what needs to change before anyone commits to building a physical version.

01
What the research told us

Healthy habits don't slip randomly. They fade at three predictable moments.

The research identified a pattern we call "the drift": the gradual slide away from healthy routines, not because youth choose to stop, but because external structure steps back before they have learned to manage it themselves. It happens at three transition points where routines reset and influence shifts.

Transition 1
P6 to Sec 1
After-school care ends. Youth get a phone, a personal device, and more pocket money. Afternoons shift from adult-supervised to self-managed before they have the skills to manage them.
Transition 2
Sec 2 to Sec 3
Peer influence becomes the dominant force. Sleep quality drops, screens fill the gap, meals get skipped because the easier option consistently wins. Without practical strategies, the pattern deepens.
Transition 3
Sec 4 to JC / IHL
External structure has largely stepped back. Youth who built their own routines carry them forward. Those who did not are still relying on someone else, but by this stage that support is gone.
02

The knowing-doing gap

Youth in this study were not uninformed. They knew fast food was not great. They knew staying up until 2am on their phones was cutting into their sleep. Several could describe a healthier routine in their own words. What many could not do was act on it, especially when they were tired, when their friends were doing something else, or when the easier option was one tap away. This is not a knowledge problem. It is a behaviour problem, and it responds to very different design decisions than information alone.

Parents had not stopped trying either. Many had kept using tools that worked well when their children were younger: rules, reminders, set routines. By Sec 2, for a number of families, those same approaches were starting to trigger resistance more than change. The care was clearly there. What was harder to find was an approach that still worked.

03
Where we want to get to

Our north star and design principles

North star
Healthy habits become the natural choice for youth, supported at key moments, shaped through shared routines, and sustained through growing independence.

Anchored to our HMW: "How might we motivate youth (aged 13 to 16) and their key supporters to build their confidence to start and sustain healthy habits across the 6 Wells (Eat, Sleep, Exercise, Learn, Bond, and Screen Time), so that healthier choices become natural, even as routines change and independence grows?"

Autonomy with guardrails

Design for shared structure, not control. Youth decide how things are done. Parents work alongside them. Neither side dictates. The structure is collaborative, which means both sides are more likely to follow it.

Replace, don't restrict

Never remove without offering something better. Screens and fast food meet real needs: comfort, stimulation, belonging. Taking them away without offering an alternative just teaches workarounds. Design what fills the space.

Make it fit the day they already have

Strengthen existing behaviours before adding new ones. Youth and parents are already busy. If something doesn't slot into the after-school hangout, the bedtime window, or the dinner table, it won't get used.

Each phase built on the last. Nothing was invented in a room. Everything was drawn from what we heard from 24 people across discovery and co-creation. 25 concept directions from youth and parents were scored against four filters. Only the ones that passed all four made it through.

05
Three concepts tested

Each responds to something specific we heard. Together they cover different windows, relationships, and wells.

C1: Bedtime Bundle

A physical magnet board on the fridge where youth design their own wind-down routine. Youth picks the magnets, parent commits their own column. Framed as a 5-night experiment with a weekly review.

Window: Night, 9pm+   Wells: Sleep, Screen, Bond   TTM: Stages 2-3

C2: Hook U Up

A physical card deck for youth buddy pairs. 5 topic decks (Glow Up, Bulk Up, Cut Season, Recharge, Feel Good), 14-day challenge, one card per day. Peer-led, no parent involvement needed.

Window: After school, 2-6pm   Wells: Eat, Exercise, Screen   TTM: Stages 3-4

C3: We Open Up

A family conversation card game with three mechanics: Category cards start the conversation, Swap has both sides answer as each other, Spark goes deeper. Both sides answer the same questions. No one is lectured.

Window: Evening, dining table   Wells: Bond (unlocks Sleep, Screen, Eat)   TTM: Stages 2-3

No two concepts target the same window or the same relationship. That is deliberate. Together they cover the gaps that open up when school ends and adult structure steps back.

Before you read on
Important: we tested physical concepts through a screen

All three concepts were designed to be held, placed, or handled. The magnet board belongs on the fridge. The card deck is meant to be shuffled and passed between friends. The conversation game is built around a simultaneous physical reveal. None of that happened in these sessions. Participants navigated prototypes on a laptop or iPad, clicking through screens instead of touching physical objects. This is a systematic constraint that affected every session. The simultaneous reveal in C3 was never reached by any participant. The magnet drag-and-drop in C1 removed the tactile dimension. The social moment of passing a card in C2 could not be replicated in an individual session with a researcher. These findings should be read with that context in mind.

Bedtime Bundle

Physical magnet board for shared sleep wind-down — Home / Bedtime
Patterns
P1
Resonated most with parents who feel the structure slipping but have not lost it completely
TP01, TP05, TP10, TP12 — across parent and youth sessions
  • TP01 (systematic Malay family, son sleeps next to her) loved it immediately and asked for a copy before being offered one. She already co-manages her son's bedtime.
  • TP05: "We've been quite systematic since young so they are kind of used to this routine." Rated it 4/7 — not because the concept failed, but because his daughter already manages herself.
  • TP10: Rated it 1/7. "Too fixed and boring." Her daughter already has an established routine. The board adds nothing new.
  • TP12 (strongest positive): "This is something we have been looking for a long time." 7/7. Single parent, daughter's sleep is a source of real tension at home.
  • TY06 (new): Can see herself using it, but some days after CCA she is exhausted and would go straight to sleep. "Some days maybe after CCA I'm very exhausted and I come back late — I wouldn't have that much time to do it."
Insight

Resonated most with parents who feel the structure slipping but have not lost it completely. TP12 is the clearest signal of who this is designed for. Where structure was already solid, participants saw no need. Where it had collapsed entirely, the concept felt too demanding to restart.

Implication

Target this at Sec 1 families in transition — the P6 to Sec 1 shift is exactly when parental oversight begins to drop and youth have not yet developed their own bedtime anchor. Reframe as a transition tool, not a general sleep aid.

Keep — physical board Fix — targeting and framing
P2
Sustainability was the primary concern — not whether participants would start, but whether they would keep going
TP07, TP09, TP10, TY11 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP07: "Will it be consistent for a period? I don't think so."
  • TP09: Said teenagers would use it for 1 to 2 weeks, then ignore it. "Kids will do it for 1 week and won't continue."
  • TP10: "The board would come down after 2 weeks." Said it needs "info bites" to sustain interest.
  • TY11: "I will keep doing it until I lose interest and forget."
  • TY06 (new): Some days after CCA she comes home late and exhausted. "Some days I might just feel lazy to do it."
Insight

The concept has no built-in re-engagement mechanism. Once novelty fades, there is nothing pulling participants back. Every age group flagged this independently. Unlike C2 where a peer nudges you, the board relies entirely on the youth's own will.

Implication

Add a lightweight re-engagement loop. Options: a rotating magnet that appears weekly, a parent-youth check-in prompt built into the Parent Says column, or a 5-night framing that resets deliberately.

Fix — weekly review mechanic Keep — 5-night trial framing
P3
The parent column role was misread by most participants — it felt like monitoring, not commitment
TP02, TP09, TY04 — 3 of 11 participants
  • TP02: Misread the board layout — thought the top section was for parents. "I thought the top part was for me to fill in."
  • TP09: Felt the children would decide what goes in the parent column. "Kids will control what goes in the parent side."
  • TY04: "Invasion of privacy — I wouldn't want my parents knowing I'm feeling sad or angry." Read the board as surveillance, not collaboration.
  • TP01 (exception): Used Parent Says naturally to write advance reminders for her son. Only one participant reached intended use without prompting.
Insight

The parent column is doing two jobs at once — tracking youth mood and showing parent commitment — and failing at both. The emotional tracking reads as one-directional rather than mutual. The concept's strongest idea (shared accountability) is not coming through.

Implication

Split the board more visibly into Youth side and Parent side with equal weight. Make the parent column explicitly about what the parent commits to, not a space to comment on the youth.

Fix — parent column design Fix — emotion section framing
P4
Always there, not a screen — that is the concept's core design advantage
TP01, TY01, TP03, TP12, TY06 — multiple sessions
  • TP01 started dragging magnets immediately without being asked. "Creates the atmosphere, creates the mood."
  • TY01: "It will help me by being more organised — I like to procrastinate a lot." The board being visible removed the need to remember to start.
  • TP05 confirmed the concept is physical, not digital, and said this was the right call — phone in the bedroom is the problem, not the solution.
  • TP12: "It's a good way for the kids to communicate to the parents. Kids nowadays don't know how to communicate."
  • TY06 (new): "If it was placed beside my bed I'd probably use it. But if it was on the fridge I'd probably forget."
Insight

The board works as a prompt even when participants are not actively thinking about sleep. This is its key behavioural advantage over any app-based solution. The physical format is not a constraint — it is the design. An unexpected function also emerged: feelings magnets let youth signal their state without needing a conversation.

Implication

Protect the physical-first principle across all iterations. Any digital companion should be opt-in and outside the bedroom. Explore the communication function of the feelings magnets. Add a "tired" option. Test placement beside the bed as an alternative to the fridge.

Keep — physical board, no app Keep — persistent visual prompt

14-day peer challenge card deck — After-school / Peer-led
Patterns
P1
4 of 5 original youth ranked C2 first — two asked to buy it without prompting
TY01, TY04, TY10, TY11, TY06, TY02 — across youth sessions

C2 is the concept most likely to spread peer-to-peer without adult initiation. Distribution should route through youth rather than parents or schools. The card deck should feel like something you find, not something you are given.

P2
The QR proof mechanic is the single biggest structural blocker
TP07, TP10, TP05, TY04 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP07: "Students not allowed phones at school as of 2026 / phone hotel." QR code cannot be scanned during school hours.
  • TP10: "Scan, log in, post, share URL." Rated ease 3/7. Youth would give up if too troublesome.
  • TY04: Wanted Duolingo-style streak instead. "I just want the streak — like Duolingo. I don't need to post anything."
  • TY08: "I wouldn't really trust giving a selfie of myself to the producers of these cards without consent." Privacy concern raised unprompted.
Insight

The QR proof does not work. Too many steps, a school phone ban from 2026, and privacy concerns from both youth and parents. Three separate blockers emerged independently. The buddy seeing it happen is enough — the peer witness is the accountability.

Implication

Replace or radically simplify. Options: a simple physical tick on the card, a shared group chat photo, or a streak sticker on the deck. The peer witness is the accountability — the platform upload is not needed.

Fix — QR proof mechanic Keep — peer accountability logic
P3
One fixed buddy for 14 days was too rigid — participants wanted flexibility
TP01, TP02, TY11 — 3 of 11 participants
  • TP01: Wanted to challenge a different person each day. "I want to be able to challenge whoever I feel like that day."
  • TP02: "If I'm the buddy it would fail — child sees me as parent, not friend."
  • TY11: "I would do this with like, whoever is around — up to five people."
Insight

The buddy system is the right social mechanic but the 14-day fixed pair is too constraining. Youth friendships are fluid. A card-by-card or week-by-week challenger model would match how teens actually socialise.

Implication

Loosen the buddy rule. Allow 2 to 4 people to rotate across 14 days. Keep the Shield mechanic. The commitment is to the streak and the group, not to one fixed person.

Fix — buddy flexibility Keep — Shield mechanic

TP10, TP04, TY04 — multiple

Rename Bulk Up. Test aspiration-consistent options: "Level Up," "Stronger," "Build." Add a skip/swap mechanic for physical challenges so injury does not block participation.

Patterns
P1
The Swap and Spark mechanic confused almost every participant — but this is a medium problem, not a concept problem
TP02, TP05, TP10 — 3 of 8 parents required researcher intervention
  • TP02: Kept trying to navigate around the blue cards without understanding what they did. Needed the researcher to intervene twice.
  • TP05: Paused when Swap and Spark cards appeared. Could not tell what they were for until the researcher gave a worked example.

Do not judge the Swap mechanic by these results. Run one physical session with real cards before deciding. In the meantime, add clearer instructions on the card itself.

P2
The concept assumes a level of trust that most families do not yet have — it needs a lighter entry point
TP10, TY04, TP09, TP07 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP10: "These are level 2 to 3 questions — need simpler daily-life set first."
  • TY04: "Would feel suspicious if parents initiated." Would prefer to play with friends first.
  • TP09: Her introverted child would shut down with emotional questions. Written notes would be less threatening.
  • TP07: Questions were "too common and general" and would only get one or two word answers.

Designed for families ready to have deeper conversations but don't know how to start. Does not account for families where even the starting point is contested, or where youth distrust the game because it came from a parent.

Add a Level 1 starter set — light, everyday, fun questions that build comfort before the deeper cards. Make the tiered structure visible. Flag weekends as the primary use window.

P3
Parents who felt distance from their child responded most strongly
TP06, TP03, TP12 — strongest; contrasted against TP10, TP07
  • TP06: Ranked C3 first. "I always say the same thing. Go sleep early. Go and study. I don't think he gets my message."

Resonated most where the relationship had already gone quieter. The reversed dynamic — youth asking parents questions — was an unexpected strength. TY06's response is notable: she would refer it to friends rather than use it herself. May need to reach families through referral, not top-down distribution.

Position C3 for the Sec 1 to Sec 2 transition window. Make the youth the initiator where possible. Amplify the reversed questioning dynamic so youth feel they are getting something out of the game.

P4
Nobody is likely to start it on their own — neither side has a natural reason to go first
TP06, TP07, TY04, TY11 — multiple participants
  • TP06: "If I don't start it, nobody will." His son would not do it on his own.
  • TY04: Said she would feel suspicious if her parents started the game. The act of initiating carries meaning.

Test a physical trigger — a card left on the table as an invitation, a conversation started by a shared meal, or a "question of the week" format. Lower the entry cost before the full game mechanic is introduced.

Across all three concepts

Five patterns that cut across C1, C2, and C3 regardless of concept. The last two have implications for how synthesis findings should be weighted.

Cross-concept 1

Online testing systematically underrepresented the physical experience. Every concept has at least one design feature that depends on holding, moving, or placing a physical object. Ratings reflect screen interactions, not product interactions.

Cross-concept 2

Youth motivation is stronger than parents predicted. Parents consistently underestimated how much their children would engage, especially with C2. Youth were more willing, specific, and creative than any parent assumed.

Cross-concept 3

All three concepts are more useful at the start of a transition than in the middle. C1 works before bedtime structure collapses. C2 before after-school time becomes habit. C3 before the relationship narrows. Timing matters as much as the concept.

Cross-concept 4

Numeric ratings did not always match what participants said. At least 4 of 16 gave scores that contradicted their verbal feedback. Ratings should be read as directional signals, not conclusive scores.

Cross-concept 5, Readiness stages

Participants sit at different stages of readiness (Transtheoretical Model). TP12 was at Preparation and responded immediately. TP10 was at Maintenance and found everything redundant. Each concept maps most naturally to a different stage. Offering the wrong concept at the wrong stage will not land regardless of design quality.

A pattern underneath the sustainability question is worth naming. Self-Determination Theory suggests that behaviour held up by external structure, whether a board on the wall, a streak counter, or a parent initiating the game, tends to fade when that structure steps back. What keeps behaviour going over time is internal motivation built on three needs: autonomy (I chose this), competence (I can do this), and relatedness (this connects me to someone). For the pilot, that means the design brief is not just "does the concept work in week one" but "does it build the kind of motivation that survives without us."

What to Pilot, Improve, or Rethink

These three concepts were built from what youth and parents said during co-creation, filtered through the project's north star, design principles, and a desirability-viability-feasibility lens. They were designed to be simple on purpose: test the underlying mechanics with real people in real conditions, not deliver polished products.

Before we assess each concept, it is worth seeing the problem they were designed to solve. The Discover-phase transcripts revealed the same cycle repeating at three key transitions. Understanding these drift loops makes clear why some mechanics worked and others did not.

The drift loops: what the transcripts showed us

At each transition, external structure falls away and the easiest option fills the gap. The cycle deepens because nothing pulls youth back toward the healthier choice. These are the loops our interventions need to break.

As-Is: The Drift
Transition 1

P6 to Sec 1: Structure falls away

1 Trigger
Student Care ends. Youth gets a phone, pocket money, and 4+ unsupervised hours. No one tells them when to eat, move, or sleep.
2 Default
Scrolls phone. Orders food delivery. Eats convenience food alone. Skips meals or snacks on bread and noodles.
3 Reward
Stimulation, comfort, feeling of independence. Zero effort required. The unhealthy choice is the easiest choice.
4 Consequence
Poor nutrition, disrupted sleep, screen dependency begins. No routine established. Parent has no visibility.
↺ Loop repeats daily. No alternative is easier, so the pattern becomes the routine.
Y09: "After school, I'm home alone. I cook dinner myself and eat alone at 7 or 8 PM."   P08: "In primary school, Student Care covered him until 7pm. Now he's on his own from 1:30pm."
Transition 2

Sec 2 to Sec 3: Peers replace parents

1 Trigger
Peer influence dominates. Parental visibility drops. Conversations that worked at age 9 now start arguments.
2 Default
Screen fills every gap. Meals are skipped. Sleep pushed later. Exercise drops because they are tired after school and CCA.
3 Reward
Social connection through screen. Autonomy from parents. The easier option wins because nothing pushes toward healthy.
4 Consequence
Sleep quality declines. Eating worsens. Parent steps back further. The pattern deepens without counter-strategies.
↺ Parent reminder triggers resistance. Youth tunes out. Distance grows. Loop accelerates.
Y04: "Every time my mom talks to me it's 'did you eat', 'go sleep', 'stop your phone.' I just tune it out now."   P09: "The conversations stopped somewhere around Sec 2."
Transition 3

Sec 4 to JC / IHL: Sink or swim

1 Trigger
External structure has stepped back. Youth is self-governing. Academic pressure peaks. No safety net remains.
2 Default
Those who built routines carry them forward. Those who did not are still relying on support that is no longer there.
3 Reward
Short-term coping feels like survival. Healthy habits are the first thing sacrificed to academic pressure.
4 Consequence
Habits are fully entrenched or absent. Exercise drops first. Sleep and eating follow. The window was Sec 1 to 3.
↺ No loop-back. By this stage the drift has either been caught or it has not.
Y06: "Gym is literally who I am now. I'll skip homework before I skip the gym."   Y12: "My parents just... stopped checking. I didn't know if they trusted me or just gave up."
To-Be: The Ideal Cycle
Transition 1

Empowered handover replaces abrupt drop

1 Same trigger
Student Care ends, phone arrives, unsupervised hours begin. The moment of choice is the same.
2 Redirected
A survival kit and parent handover toolkit make the healthier option the path of least resistance. Structure is replaced, not removed.
3 Felt reward
"I managed my own afternoon." Autonomy becomes the reward — the same need, met through a healthier route.
4 Confidence
Each successful afternoon builds the belief: "I can handle this myself." Parent shifts from managing to coaching.
↺ Routine forms before the drift takes hold. Structure becomes self-generated.
Transition 2

Peers become the engine, not the obstacle

1 Same trigger
Peer influence dominates. Parent reminders stop working. The same social pressure that drove drift is still there.
2 Redirected
Group challenges and streaks turn peer pressure into peer accountability. The social hook drives healthy behaviour instead of screen default.
3 Felt reward
"My streak grew," "my squad noticed," "I look better." Identity-linked wins that matter more than a health lecture.
4 Confidence
The healthy choice becomes part of group identity. Breaking the streak costs more than continuing it. Confidence compounds socially.
↺ Social proof sustains the loop. Each person's streak reinforces the group.
Transition 3

Youth who built routines carry them forward

1 Same trigger
External structure is gone. Academic pressure peaks. Same conditions as the drift — but routines are already in place.
2 Self-directed
The intervention is no longer needed. Shared rhythms with family provide a stable base. Youth own their choices.
3 Felt reward
"I know what works for me." The reward is internal — competence and self-knowledge built across Sec 1 to 3.
4 Resilience
When life disrupts the routine, they restart instead of giving up. The healthy choice is the obvious choice.
↺ The loop sustains itself. Each disruption is a restart, not a failure.

Tools and Frameworks Used

Every framework in this report was chosen for a specific reason. We did not apply them because they are standard practice. We applied them because each one answers a question that the others cannot. This page explains what each tool does, why we used it, and where it appears in the report.

Anchor framework
Transtheoretical Model (TTM)
Stages of Change: Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance
A behaviour change model that describes five stages people move through when adopting a new habit. Not everyone is at the same starting point. Some youth have not yet thought about changing their routine. Others are already planning to act. A few are already doing it. The stage a person is at determines what kind of support will actually land, and what will feel irrelevant or even patronising. TTM was the first lens we applied to our participants. Before evaluating any concept, we needed to know where each person was standing.
Why this is the anchor
Every other framework in this report answers a downstream question. EAST asks "will they adopt it?" B=MAP asks "where does it break?" Kano asks "what to prioritise?" SDT asks "will it last?" But none of those questions matter if you are offering the intervention to someone who is not ready for it. TTM is the first question: where is this person right now? It determined how we read every participant's response. TP12 gave C1 a 7/7 not because C1 is a better concept, but because TP12 was at Stage 3 (Preparation), actively looking for exactly this kind of tool. TP10 gave C1 a 1/7 not because C1 is flawed, but because TP10 was at Stage 5 (Maintenance), already had a working routine, and found the concept redundant. Without TTM, those two scores look like a contradiction. With TTM, they are both correct. The concept is fine. The targeting is what matters. This is also why the research identified three transition points (P6 to Sec 1, Sec 2 to Sec 3, Sec 4 to JC) as the moments where the drift happens. Those transitions are when people move between stages, and that is when the intervention has the best chance of landing.

7-Point Likert Scale
Quantitative self-report rating
Each participant rated every concept they saw on a 1-to-7 scale across five dimensions: overall experience, likelihood of use, ease of understanding, perceived pressure or control, and fit with daily routines. The scale gives a directional number that can be compared across concepts and participants.
Why we used it
Qualitative feedback tells you what people think. A rating tells you how strongly they feel about it. We needed both. The Likert scale gave us a way to spot outliers quickly (TP10 rating 1/7 on C1 while TP12 rated 7/7) and to compare concepts side by side. However, we also found that ratings did not always match what participants said, which is itself useful information. At least 4 of 16 participants gave scores that contradicted their verbal feedback. That is why we treat ratings as directional signals, not conclusions.

Pre-Set Success Metrics
Concept-level pass/fail criteria
Before testing began, we defined 3 to 4 success metrics per concept. These were specific, observable outcomes: "Youth chooses magnets that reflect what they would actually do" or "Proof step does not break the streak." After testing, each metric was scored as Met, Partial, or Not Met based on what participants actually did and said.
Why we used it
Setting success criteria before seeing results prevents the team from moving the goalposts after the fact. It forces clarity about what "working" actually means for each concept. Without pre-set metrics, it is easy to rationalise mixed results as positive because something landed well. With them, you know exactly what you were looking for and whether you found it. This is especially important when the client asks "will it work?" The answer is not a feeling. It is a checklist that was written before the data came in.

EAST
Easy, Attractive, Social, Timely
A behavioural science framework developed by the UK Behavioural Insights Team. It evaluates whether an intervention is Easy to do, Attractive to engage with, Social in nature (uses peer or group dynamics), and Timely in when it reaches people. Each concept is scored against all four dimensions.
Why we used it
EAST answers the question: "Even if people want to use this, will they actually do it?" A concept can be desirable and still fail because it is too hard to start, not appealing enough to try, isolating instead of social, or delivered at the wrong moment. EAST catches those friction points. For example, C2 scored well on Attractive and Social but poorly on Easy because the QR proof mechanic had too many steps. Without EAST, we might have said "youth like it" and missed that the proof step would kill it in practice.
Used in: Step 3: Assessment (all concepts)
B=MAP
Behaviour = Motivation × Ability × Prompt
From BJ Fogg's Behavior Model. A behaviour happens when three things come together at the same moment: the person is motivated enough, they have the ability to do it, and something prompts them to act. If any one of the three is missing, the behaviour does not happen, regardless of how strong the other two are.
Why we used it
B=MAP tells you exactly where a concept breaks. Is the problem that youth do not want to do it (motivation)? That they want to but cannot figure out how (ability)? Or that nothing reminds them to start (prompt)? Each failure point requires a different fix. C1's prompt was its strongest feature (a physical board on the fridge is always visible), but its motivation was present for only a subset of families. C3 had motivation from parents but zero prompt: nothing makes either side pick up the cards. Knowing which component fails tells the design team where to focus, instead of redesigning everything.
Used in: Step 3: Assessment (all concepts)
Kano Model
Must-have / Performance / Delighter classification
A product design framework that sorts features into three categories. Must-haves are features that people expect and notice only when they are missing (like text labels on icons). Performance features improve satisfaction the more you invest in them (like the number of challenge decks). Delighters are unexpected features that create strong positive reactions (like the feelings magnets doubling as a communication tool).
Why we used it
Kano answers the question: "Where should we spend our limited budget?" Not all features are equal. Fixing a must-have (text labels on C1's emotion icons) removes a barrier. Improving a performance feature (adding more challenge decks to C2) increases engagement. Building a delighter (feelings magnets as communication) creates advocacy. If you spend resources polishing a delighter while a must-have is broken, the concept still fails. Kano prevents that by making the priority order visible.
Used in: Step 3: Assessment (all concepts)
Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness
A motivation theory that identifies three psychological needs which, when met, produce internal motivation that sustains over time. Autonomy means feeling that you chose this, not that someone imposed it. Competence means feeling capable of doing it. Relatedness means feeling connected to others through the behaviour. When all three are present, motivation tends to persist even after external support is removed.
Why we used it
The sustainability question kept coming up: "Will youth still do this after the novelty fades?" SDT answers that directly. Behaviour held up by external structure (a board on the wall, a streak counter, a parent initiating a game) tends to fade when that structure steps back. What keeps behaviour going is internal motivation built on autonomy, competence, and relatedness. SDT helped explain why C2's motivation held without adult involvement (youth chose their own decks, felt competent, and connected to a buddy) while C3's motivation split along role lines (parents felt the relatedness pull but youth did not experience autonomy in starting the game).

Keep / Fix / Drop
Design action classification
A simple sorting tool that classifies every finding into one of three actions. Keep means the feature works as designed and should be protected in the next iteration. Fix means the feature has value but needs changes before it is usable. Drop means the feature actively harms the experience and should be removed.
Why we used it
Research produces a lot of findings. Without a clear action label, teams tend to treat everything as "interesting" and either change too much or not enough. Keep/Fix/Drop forces a decision on every pattern: is this something to protect, something to redesign, or something to remove? It makes the design brief for the next iteration concrete. For this report, 35% of design elements were Keep, 61% were Fix, and 4% were Drop. That tells the team that most of the design logic is sound but the execution needs targeted work.

DVF Scoring
Desirability, Viability, Feasibility
A concept selection framework that scores ideas against three lenses. Desirability asks whether people actually want it. Viability asks whether the organisation can sustain it (budget, channels, staffing). Feasibility asks whether it can be built and delivered within the constraints. An idea needs to pass all three to move forward.
Why we used it
DVF was used before this report, during concept selection. The 25 ideas from co-creation were scored against DVF alongside the north star, design principles, and design constraints. Only the ideas that passed all four filters were brought forward for prototyping. This matters for context: the three concepts tested in this report were not arbitrary picks. They were the survivors of a structured selection process that considered whether people want it, whether HPB can run it, and whether it can reach enough people within the project's constraints (no standalone apps, existing channels, usable at home).

Pattern Strength Count
Independent participant convergence
For every pattern identified in the synthesis, we counted how many participants independently raised the same observation without being prompted. This is not a statistical measure. It is a convergence signal: when multiple people who have never met each other describe the same experience, that pattern is more likely to reflect something real than one person's preference.
Why we used it
Qualitative research is sometimes dismissed as "just opinions." Pattern strength turns individual observations into evidence. When 5 out of 11 participants independently flag sustainability as a concern for C1, that is not one person's worry. It is a signal. We treat 4 or more participants as a strong signal and 2 or fewer as a hypothesis worth watching. This gives the client a way to weigh findings without needing a statistically significant sample, which is not the purpose of qualitative research in the first place.
