HPB Youth Preventive Services

Validation Testing Report

We tested three early-stage concepts with 21 participants — 12 parents and 9 youth — over multiple online sessions in March 2026. Each concept tackles a different moment in a young person's day: bedtime, after-school time with friends, and family conversation at home. None of them were finished products. They were prototypes, built just enough to put in front of real people and find out what holds up and what falls apart.

The goal was practical: understand whether each concept's core mechanic actually works when someone who has never seen it before tries to use it. We wanted to know what resonates, what confuses, and what needs to change before anyone commits to building a physical version. We also wanted to see what patterns cut across all three ideas — things about how youth and parents relate to health behaviours that no single concept can tell you on its own.

This report is organised in three steps. The first is the raw evidence — what participants actually said and did, organised by concept. The second is the synthesis — where we pull out the patterns, name what each one means, and say what it implies for the design. The third is the assessment — concept-level scores, behavioural framework analysis, and prioritised fixes. You can read it end to end or jump straight to the section that matters most to you.

Three concepts tested

C1 — Bedtime Bundle

A physical magnet board placed in the bedroom. Youth pick magnets that reflect their wind-down routine, and parents commit their own column. Framed as a 5-night experiment with a weekly review.

C2 — Hook U Up

A physical card deck with 14-day peer challenges across five topic packs: eating, exercise, screen habits, sleep, and social connection. Designed for the after-school window, led by friends rather than parents.

C3 — We Open Up

A family conversation card game played at the dining table. Uses question cards with a Swap and Spark mechanic to create moments of unexpected honesty between parents and youth.

C1

Bedtime Bundle

Physical magnet board for shared sleep wind-down — Home / Bedtime
Patterns
P1
Resonated most with parents who feel the structure slipping but have not lost it completely
TP01, TP05, TP10, TP12 — across parent and youth sessions
  • TP01 (systematic Malay family, son sleeps next to her) loved it immediately and asked for a copy before being offered one. She already co-manages her son's bedtime.
  • TP05: "We've been quite systematic since young so they are kind of used to this routine." Rated it 4/7 — not because the concept failed, but because his daughter already manages herself.
  • TP10: Rated it 1/7. "Too fixed and boring." Her daughter already has an established routine. The board adds nothing new.
  • TP12 (strongest positive): "This is something we have been looking for a long time." 7/7. Single parent, daughter's sleep is a source of real tension at home.
  • TY06 (new): Can see herself using it, but some days after CCA she is exhausted and would go straight to sleep. "Some days maybe after CCA I'm very exhausted and I come back late — I wouldn't have that much time to do it."
Insight

Resonated most with parents who feel the structure slipping but have not lost it completely. TP12 is the clearest signal of who this is designed for. Where structure was already solid, participants saw no need. Where it had collapsed entirely, the concept felt too demanding to restart.

Implication

Target this at Sec 1 families in transition — the P6 to Sec 1 shift is exactly when parental oversight begins to drop and youth have not yet developed their own bedtime anchor. Reframe as a transition tool, not a general sleep aid.

Keep — physical board Fix — targeting and framing
P2
Sustainability was the primary concern — not whether participants would start, but whether they would keep going
TP07, TP09, TP10, TY11 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP07: "Will it be consistent for a period? I don't think so."
  • TP09: Said teenagers would use it for 1 to 2 weeks, then ignore it. "Kids will do it for 1 week and won't continue."
  • TP10: "The board would come down after 2 weeks." Said it needs "info bites" to sustain interest.
  • TY11: "I will keep doing it until I lose interest and forget."
  • TY06 (new): Some days after CCA she comes home late and exhausted. "Some days I might just feel lazy to do it."
Insight

The concept has no built-in re-engagement mechanism. Once novelty fades, there is nothing pulling participants back. Every age group flagged this independently. Unlike C2 where a peer nudges you, the board relies entirely on the youth's own will.

Implication

Add a lightweight re-engagement loop. Options: a rotating magnet that appears weekly, a parent-youth check-in prompt built into the Parent Says column, or a 5-night framing that resets deliberately.

Fix — weekly review mechanic Keep — 5-night trial framing
P3
The parent column role was misread by most participants — it felt like monitoring, not commitment
TP02, TP09, TY04 — 3 of 11 participants
  • TP02: Misread the board layout — thought the top section was for parents. "I thought the top part was for me to fill in."
  • TP09: Felt the children would decide what goes in the parent column. "Kids will control what goes in the parent side."
  • TY04: "Invasion of privacy — I wouldn't want my parents knowing I'm feeling sad or angry." Read the board as surveillance, not collaboration.
  • TP01 (exception): Used Parent Says naturally to write advance reminders for her son. Only one participant reached intended use without prompting.
Insight

The parent column is doing two jobs at once — tracking youth mood and showing parent commitment — and failing at both. The emotional tracking reads as one-directional rather than mutual. The concept's strongest idea (shared accountability) is not coming through.

Implication

Split the board more visibly into Youth side and Parent side with equal weight. Make the parent column explicitly about what the parent commits to, not a space to comment on the youth.

Fix — parent column design Fix — emotion section framing
P4
Always there, not a screen — that is the concept's core design advantage
TP01, TY01, TP03, TP12, TY06 — multiple sessions
  • TP01 started dragging magnets immediately without being asked. "Creates the atmosphere, creates the mood."
  • TY01: "It will help me by being more organised — I like to procrastinate a lot." The board being visible removed the need to remember to start.
  • TP05 confirmed the concept is physical, not digital, and said this was the right call — phone in the bedroom is the problem, not the solution.
  • TP12: "It's a good way for the kids to communicate to the parents. Kids nowadays don't know how to communicate."
  • TY06 (new): "If it was placed beside my bed I'd probably use it. But if it was on the fridge I'd probably forget."
Insight

The board works as a prompt even when participants are not actively thinking about sleep. This is its key behavioural advantage over any app-based solution. The physical format is not a constraint — it is the design. An unexpected function also emerged: feelings magnets let youth signal their state without needing a conversation.

Implication

Protect the physical-first principle across all iterations. Any digital companion should be opt-in and outside the bedroom. Explore the communication function of the feelings magnets. Add a "tired" option. Test placement beside the bed as an alternative to the fridge.

Keep — physical board, no app Keep — persistent visual prompt
C2

Hook U Up

14-day peer challenge card deck — After-school / Peer-led
Patterns
P1
4 of 5 original youth ranked C2 first — two asked to buy it without prompting
TY01, TY04, TY10, TY11, TY06, TY02 — across youth sessions
  • TY01: Rated 7/7 all dimensions. "Would keep me occupied for hours." Said she would pay for it.
  • TY04: Asked mid-session how to buy it. No prompting. "Can I buy this? Where can I get it?"
  • TY10: Ranked C2 first. "I would not mind doing this with my friends actually."
  • TY11: Invented her own bet mechanic — "loser buys the winner something small" — without suggestion.
  • TY06 (new): "It's kind of like Snapchat or TikTok streaks — you don't want to break the streak."
  • TY08 (outlier): Under O-level pressure. "Might give stress to those who overthink." Hesitation about exam pressure, not the concept.
Insight

The concept has real pull with youth independently of parents or schools. The peer mechanic and the language (energy era, glow up, rizz) make health feel social and identity-relevant rather than instructional. It asks youth to care about their social world — the health outcome follows.

Implication

C2 is the concept most likely to spread peer-to-peer without adult initiation. Distribution should route through youth rather than parents or schools. The card deck should feel like something you find, not something you are given.

Keep — peer-led mechanic Keep — identity-first language
P2
The QR proof mechanic is the single biggest structural blocker
TP07, TP10, TP05, TY04 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP07: "Students not allowed phones at school as of 2026 / phone hotel." QR code cannot be scanned during school hours.
  • TP10: "Scan, log in, post, share URL." Rated ease 3/7. Youth would give up if too troublesome.
  • TY04: Wanted Duolingo-style streak instead. "I just want the streak — like Duolingo. I don't need to post anything."
  • TY08: "I wouldn't really trust giving a selfie of myself to the producers of these cards without consent." Privacy concern raised unprompted.
Insight

The QR proof does not work. Too many steps, a school phone ban from 2026, and privacy concerns from both youth and parents. Three separate blockers emerged independently. The buddy seeing it happen is enough — the peer witness is the accountability.

Implication

Replace or radically simplify. Options: a simple physical tick on the card, a shared group chat photo, or a streak sticker on the deck. The peer witness is the accountability — the platform upload is not needed.

Fix — QR proof mechanic Keep — peer accountability logic
P3
One fixed buddy for 14 days was too rigid — participants wanted flexibility
TP01, TP02, TY11 — 3 of 11 participants
  • TP01: Wanted to challenge a different person each day. "I want to be able to challenge whoever I feel like that day."
  • TP02: "If I'm the buddy it would fail — child sees me as parent, not friend."
  • TY11: "I would do this with like, whoever is around — up to five people."
Insight

The buddy system is the right social mechanic but the 14-day fixed pair is too constraining. Youth friendships are fluid. A card-by-card or week-by-week challenger model would match how teens actually socialise.

Implication

Loosen the buddy rule. Allow 2 to 4 people to rotate across 14 days. Keep the Shield mechanic. The commitment is to the streak and the group, not to one fixed person.

Fix — buddy flexibility Keep — Shield mechanic
P4
"Bulk Up" was the only deck name that put participants off — the others landed immediately
TP10, TP04, TY04 — multiple
  • TP10: "Bulk Up puts girls off — it sounds like it's only for guys who want muscles."
  • TY04: Could not do physical challenges because of her ACL injury. A skip or swap option was missing.
  • Glow Up, Recharge, and Feel Good resonated without explanation. Participants understood these instantly.
  • TY02 (new): "What are the benefits that the body is gaining? That's what's missing."
Insight

"Bulk Up" carries a body-building connotation that does not match the language of female participants. The other four deck names use aspiration language — Bulk Up is the only one that uses effort language.

Implication

Rename Bulk Up. Test aspiration-consistent options: "Level Up," "Stronger," "Build." Add a skip/swap mechanic for physical challenges so injury does not block participation.

Fix — rename Bulk Up Fix — add skip/swap Keep — other four deck names
C3

We Open Up

Family conversation card game — Home / Dining table
Patterns
P1
The Swap and Spark mechanic confused almost every participant — but this is a medium problem, not a concept problem
TP02, TP05, TP10 — 3 of 8 parents required researcher intervention
  • TP02: Kept trying to navigate around the blue cards without understanding what they did. Needed the researcher to intervene twice.
  • TP05: Paused when Swap and Spark cards appeared. Could not tell what they were for until the researcher gave a worked example.
  • TP10: Caught the Spark card design flaw herself — "the question doesn't link to the category card."
  • No participant reached the simultaneous reveal in any session. The mechanic was never experienced as intended.
Insight

Clicking a card on a screen is not the same as picking one up. The Swap mechanic depends on physical simultaneity. Online, there is no way to recreate this. The confusion is almost certainly a testing format problem, not a concept failure.

Implication

Do not judge the Swap mechanic by these results. Run one physical session with real cards before deciding. In the meantime, add clearer instructions on the card itself.

Keep — simultaneous reveal Fix — on-card instructions
P2
The concept assumes a level of trust that most families do not yet have — it needs a lighter entry point
TP10, TY04, TP09, TP07 — 4 of 11 participants
  • TP10: "These are level 2 to 3 questions — need simpler daily-life set first."
  • TY04: "Would feel suspicious if parents initiated." Would prefer to play with friends first.
  • TP09: Her introverted child would shut down with emotional questions. Written notes would be less threatening.
  • TP07: Questions were "too common and general" and would only get one or two word answers.
Insight

Designed for families ready to have deeper conversations but don't know how to start. Does not account for families where even the starting point is contested, or where youth distrust the game because it came from a parent.

Implication

Add a Level 1 starter set — light, everyday, fun questions that build comfort before the deeper cards. Make the tiered structure visible. Flag weekends as the primary use window.

Fix — add Level 1 starter set Keep — deeper question set for later
P3
Parents who felt distance from their child responded most strongly
TP06, TP03, TP12 — strongest; contrasted against TP10, TP07
  • TP06: Ranked C3 first. "I always say the same thing. Go sleep early. Go and study. I don't think he gets my message."
  • TY01: Found the reversed dynamic exciting. "The game lets me ask questions TO my parents — that's kind of unusual." Rated 7/7.
  • TP03: "Harmless looking segway to deeper conversations."
  • TY06 (new): "Some of my friends don't have a very close relationship with their parents. I thought this might help a bit." A referral signal.
Insight

Resonated most where the relationship had already gone quieter. The reversed dynamic — youth asking parents questions — was an unexpected strength. TY06's response is notable: she would refer it to friends rather than use it herself. May need to reach families through referral, not top-down distribution.

Implication

Position C3 for the Sec 1 to Sec 2 transition window. Make the youth the initiator where possible. Amplify the reversed questioning dynamic so youth feel they are getting something out of the game.

Keep — reversed questioning Fix — reduce initiation friction
P4
Nobody is likely to start it on their own — neither side has a natural reason to go first
TP06, TP07, TY04, TY11 — multiple participants
  • TP06: "If I don't start it, nobody will." His son would not do it on his own.
  • TY04: Said she would feel suspicious if her parents started the game. The act of initiating carries meaning.
  • TY11: "Fun is necessary to maintain engagement."
  • Unlike C1 where the board is always visible and C2 where a friend nudges you, C3 sits in a drawer until someone decides to take it out.
Insight

The prompt problem is structural. C3 needs someone to decide to play it, and that decision is loaded — for youth it can feel like a setup, for parents it can feel awkward. The concept needs a low-stakes trigger that makes starting feel incidental rather than deliberate.

Implication

Test a physical trigger — a card left on the table as an invitation, a conversation started by a shared meal, or a "question of the week" format. Lower the entry cost before the full game mechanic is introduced.

Fix — add low-stakes trigger Fix — make starting feel incidental

Across all three concepts

Five patterns that cut across C1, C2, and C3 regardless of concept. The last two have implications for how synthesis findings should be weighted.

Cross-concept 1

Online testing systematically underrepresented the physical experience. Every concept has at least one design feature that depends on holding, moving, or placing a physical object. Ratings reflect screen interactions, not product interactions.

Cross-concept 2

Youth motivation is stronger than parents predicted. Parents consistently underestimated how much their children would engage, especially with C2. Youth were more willing, specific, and creative than any parent assumed.

Cross-concept 3

All three concepts are more useful at the start of a transition than in the middle. C1 works before bedtime structure collapses. C2 before after-school time becomes habit. C3 before the relationship narrows. Timing matters as much as the concept.

Cross-concept 4

Numeric ratings did not always match what participants said. At least 4 of 16 gave scores that contradicted their verbal feedback. Ratings should be read as directional signals, not conclusive scores.

Cross-concept 5 — Readiness stages

Participants sit at different stages of readiness (Transtheoretical Model). TP12 was at Preparation and responded immediately. TP10 was at Maintenance and found everything redundant. Each concept maps most naturally to a different stage. Offering the wrong concept at the wrong stage will not land regardless of design quality.
